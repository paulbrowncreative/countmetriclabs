What ISO 11171 is

ISO 11171, Hydraulic fluid power — Calibration of automatic particle counters for liquids, is the international standard for calibrating automatic particle counters (APCs) that analyze bottle samples of liquid. It's maintained by ISO technical committee TC 131/SC 6, the same group responsible for the ISO 4406 cleanliness code. The current edition is ISO 11171:2022.

The standard covers primary and secondary particle-sizing calibration for sizes of 1 µm(c) and larger, sensor resolution and counting performance, acceptable operating limits, sensor verification with test dust, and coincidence and flow-rate limits. It applies to hydraulic fluids, aviation and diesel fuels, engine oils and other petroleum-based fluids.

In short: ISO 11171 is what makes a particle count from one instrument comparable with a count from another.

Why it replaced ISO 4402

Before ISO 11171, particle counters were calibrated to ISO 4402 using AC Fine Test Dust (ACFTD). That approach had two problems. ACFTD stopped being produced in the early 1990s, and its size distribution had been established by optical microscopy measuring each particle's longest dimension, which couldn't be traced to a national measurement standard.

ISO 11171 replaced it with ISO Medium Test Dust (ISO 12103-1, A3) and, critically, with a certified reference material from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology: NIST Standard Reference Material 2806, a suspension of medium test dust in hydraulic fluid with a certified particle size distribution. That certification is what gives ISO 11171 calibrations their traceability.

What µm(c) means

The "(c)" in µm(c) indicates a particle size measured with an APC calibrated to ISO 11171. NIST certifies SRM 2806 in terms of the projected-area equivalent circle diameter of the particles, so a size of 6 µm(c) refers to a particle whose projected area equals that of a 6 µm circle.

Because the sizing basis changed, sizes under the old and new methods don't match one-for-one. When ISO 4406 was revised to use ISO 11171 sizes, its reporting channels moved to 4, 6 and 14 µm(c), chosen so that codes remain broadly comparable with those produced at the old 2, 5 and 15 µm channels.

Practical consequence: if your instrument or a supplier's lab still reports to the old method, cleanliness codes aren't directly comparable with ISO 11171-based results. Ask which method produced any code you're relying on.

Primary vs secondary calibration

ISO 11171 defines two routes to a traceable sizing calibration. Both are legitimate. They differ in the reference suspension used.

Primary and secondary calibration compared

ReferenceNIST SRM 2806 suspensionCalibration suspensions whose size distribution was verified with a primary-calibrated APC

Cost and availabilityHigher; SRM supply is limitedLower; suited to routine work
Common useReference instruments and labs that produce secondary suspensionsRoutine calibration of working lab and portable counters

For most working instruments, a properly documented secondary calibration is the norm. What matters is that the chain back to NIST is recorded: which suspension, which lot, and how it was verified.

What a full ISO 11171 calibration covers

Sizing is the headline, but a calibration that stops at the curve leaves important questions unanswered. A complete ISO 11171 calibration also establishes whether the instrument is fit to use and under what conditions.

  • Preliminary checks. Sample volume accuracy and threshold noise level, so the measurement system itself isn't distorting results.
  • Sizing calibration. A calibration curve relating the sensor's threshold settings to particle size in µm(c).
  • Resolution. How sharply the sensor distinguishes particles of similar size. Poor resolution smears counts between channels.
  • Coincidence error limit. The concentration above which two or more particles in the sensing zone at once are counted as one larger particle. Samples above this limit must be diluted.
  • Flow-rate limits. The range of flow rates over which sizing and counting remain within tolerance.
  • Sensor performance verification. A check with test dust that the calibrated instrument counts as expected.

The exact procedures and acceptance criteria are in the standard itself, which is available from ISO and national standards bodies.

What your calibration report should show

If a customer, auditor or warranty investigator asks how a cleanliness code was produced, the calibration report is your evidence. At minimum, look for:

  • The ISO 11171 edition used and whether calibration was primary or secondary
  • Identity and lot numbers of the reference suspensions, with a traceability statement
  • The calibration curve or threshold settings for each size channel
  • Measured resolution, coincidence limit and working flow-rate range
  • As-found results before adjustment, and as-left results after
  • Instrument identity, date, conditions and the people who performed and reviewed the work

A certificate that shows only "PASS" and a date gives you very little to defend a result with.

How often should a particle counter be calibrated?

Many quality programs use an annual interval. Recalibration is also prudent after a sensor is repaired or replaced, after significant changes to the fluid system, or whenever verification checks or comparisons suggest drift. Your own quality procedure, or your customer's requirement, is what governs.

The as-found result from each calibration is the best evidence for whether your interval is right. If as-found results are consistently within limits, your interval is working. If they're not, shorten it.

What about online particle counters?

ISO 11171 addresses APCs that analyze bottle samples. Online automatic particle counting systems, including those used in filter testing, are covered by ISO 11943, which describes methods for online secondary calibration and validation.
