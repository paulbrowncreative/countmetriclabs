Sampling guidance

Most bad particle counts start at the sample valve.

A laboratory can only measure what's in the bottle. These practices reflect widely used guidance, including ISO 4021 for sampling from operating systems and ISO 3722 for bottle cleanliness.

  1. Use a certified clean bottle

    Bottle cleanliness should be verified, typically to ISO 3722. A dirty bottle adds particles you'll be blamed for.

  2. Sample a running system

    Take the sample from a live, turbulent zone with the system at normal operating conditions, not from a stagnant reservoir corner or drain.

  3. Flush the valve first

    Let fluid run through the sampling valve before filling, so you capture system fluid rather than what settled in the valve.

  4. Leave air space

    Fill to roughly three-quarters so the lab can agitate the sample and resuspend particles evenly.

  5. Cap and label immediately

    Record the equipment ID, sample point, date, fluid type and whether the system was running.

  6. Ship promptly

    Send samples soon after collection. Tell us if the fluid is unusual, dark, water-containing or hazardous.

Accuracy

What can distort a particle count, and how we handle it

Light-extinction counters can't tell a particle from anything else that blocks light. Part of our job is making sure they don't have to.

Air bubbles

Entrained air counts as particles. Samples are degassed before analysis so bubbles don't inflate the result.

Free water

Water droplets are counted as particles. We flag visibly wet samples and discuss the options with you before reporting.

High concentration

Above the counter's coincidence limit, particles overlap and are undercounted. Heavily contaminated samples are diluted with clean fluid, and the dilution is reported.

Dark or opaque fluids

Light extinction needs a clear, single-phase liquid. Dark fluids may need dilution, or a different method. We'll tell you which before testing.

FAQ

Testing questions

Do you supply sample bottles?

Ask when you request a quote. We'll confirm the bottle type and cleanliness level suitable for your target code, and how to get them.

Can you test a sample that another lab already tested?

Yes, and it's one of the most useful things we do. If two labs report different codes for the same fluid, send us a sample and both reports. Our consulting service can also help work out why they disagree.

What cleanliness code should my system run at?

Your equipment manufacturer's target is the starting point, and it usually depends on the most sensitive component and the operating pressure. If you don't have a target, we can help you set one as part of a consulting engagement.

Why is my ISO 4406 code marked with "≥"?

It indicates that very few particles were counted at that size, so the result carries more statistical uncertainty. Our ISO 4406 guide explains when this applies.
