Why it matters

An uncalibrated counter turns a cleanliness spec into a guess.

An automatic particle counter doesn't measure particle size directly. It measures how much light each particle blocks and converts that signal to a size using a calibration curve. If the curve drifts, every size channel shifts with it, and an ISO 4406 code can move by one or more scale numbers without the fluid changing at all.

ISO 11171 defines how that curve is established and how the rest of the instrument is proven fit to use: sensor resolution, the concentration at which particles start to overlap in the sensor, and the flow rates at which counts stay stable. Sizing is traceable to NIST Standard Reference Material 2806, which is why a calibrated "4 µm(c)" means the same thing on every compliant instrument.

When a filter acceptance test, a supplier audit or a warranty claim turns on a cleanliness code, this is the paper trail that makes the number defensible.

Scope

What an ISO 11171 calibration includes

The elements below follow the structure of ISO 11171. We confirm the exact scope for your instrument in writing before you ship.

  1. As-found check

    We record how your instrument performs as received, before any adjustment. This tells you whether results reported since the last calibration are affected.

  2. Preliminary checks

    Sample volume accuracy and threshold noise level are verified so later results aren't distorted by the measurement system itself.

  3. Sizing calibration

    Certified calibration suspensions establish the curve relating sensor threshold settings to particle size in µm(c), traceable to NIST SRM 2806.

  4. Resolution

    Sensor resolution is determined and compared with ISO 11171 limits. Poor resolution blurs adjacent size channels.

  5. Coincidence and flow-rate limits

    We establish the maximum concentration you can count without significant coincidence error, and the flow-rate range over which sizing holds.

  6. Performance verification and as-left

    Sensor performance is verified with test dust, and final as-left results are recorded alongside the as-found data.

Calibration type

Primary or secondary calibration?

Both are defined in ISO 11171 and both are traceable to NIST. The difference is the reference suspension used.

Before you ship

How to prepare your instrument

Ten minutes of preparation avoids delays and makes the as-found result meaningful.

Don't ship until we've confirmed fit. Send the make and model first. We'll reply with packing and shipping instructions specific to your instrument.

  • Flush the sensor with clean, filtered fluid compatible with your instrument, and drain it before packing.
  • Don't adjust anything first. Leave settings as they are so the as-found check reflects how you've been measuring.
  • Include what the instrument needs to run: bottle sampler, power supply, cables and any required software or licence.
  • Note recent problems, such as error messages, unstable counts or a disagreement with another lab.
  • Send your last report if you have one, so we can compare against previous results.
  • Pack it for a precision instrument: original case if available, sensor ports capped.

FAQ

Calibration questions

Can you calibrate online or in-line particle counters?

ISO 11171 covers APCs that analyze bottle samples. Online systems are calibrated and validated using the methods in ISO 11943. Tell us the model and how it's installed, and we'll tell you whether we can help.

What happens if my instrument fails the as-found check?

We record the as-found result, recalibrate, and record the as-left result. The report shows both, so you can decide whether results since the last calibration need review. If the instrument has a fault calibration can't correct, we'll tell you before going further.

Which fluids does ISO 11171 apply to?

The current edition applies to hydraulic fluids, aviation and diesel fuels, engine oils and other petroleum-based fluids. If you count particles in something else, ask us about method fit before booking.

Will my reported codes change after calibration?

They can. If your instrument had drifted, or was last calibrated to the obsolete ISO 4402 method, counts at each size channel may shift. The as-found and as-left data show you by how much.

Is the calibration accredited?

Not yet. Our reports state the method and traceability, and carry no accreditation mark. If your contract specifically requires an accredited calibration, tell us and we'll be upfront about whether we can meet it today. Read our status.
